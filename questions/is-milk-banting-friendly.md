**Short answer:** Only in tiny amounts. Full-cream milk is a medium-carb food: a splash in tea or coffee is fine, but large amounts can slow weight loss for some people. Skim and low-fat milk are high-carb. Full-cream milk carries 4.8 g net carbs per 100 ml, so one 250 ml glass costs you 12 g net carbs.

| Per 100 ml | Total carbs | Fibre | Net carbs | Fat | Protein | Carb band |
|------------|-------------|-------|-----------|-----|---------|--------------|
| Milk, full cream | 4.8 g | 0 g | 4.8 g | 3.3 g | 3.2 g | Medium-carb |
| Fresh cream (whipping) | 2.8 g | 0 g | 2.8 g | 35 g | 2.1 g | Low-carb |
| Milk, skim / low-fat | 5.0 g | 0 g | 5.0 g | 0.1–1.5 g | 3.4 g | High-carb |

A 250 ml glass has about 12 g total carbs, 0 g fibre and **12 g net carbs**. A 30 ml splash in coffee has about **1.4 g net carbs**.

Source: USDA FoodData Central.

## What to watch for

Milk sugar (lactose) is the whole problem — there's no fibre to blunt it, and low-fat milk is even worse for insulin spikes. Full-cream milk lists around 4.8 g glycaemic carbs per 100 ml — check the pack you buy. "Low fat" and "fat free" versions strip the natural fat that slows down digestion, landing them in the high-carb group. Flavoured milks are far worse: a 250 ml strawberry or chocolate milk can hit 25–30 g carbs, basically liquid dessert.

Count your cups. Two big milky coffees plus a glass of milk with supper can quietly eat 20 g of your ~25–50 g daily budget before you've touched food. I logged exactly that mistake in Banting Buddy when I started — three "harmless" coffees, 9 g carbs gone, and I couldn't work out why my totals looked high.

What works: a small splash (a tablespoon or two) in tea or coffee, which costs 1–2 g and keeps the taste you like. What doesn't: milky oats-style porridge made with two cups of milk, "recovery" chocolate milk after gym, or cereal bowls drowned in milk. If you love a big hot drink, make an "Americano" with hot water and a tablespoon of fresh cream instead — richer taste, and only ~0.5 g carbs.

## What to eat instead

Fresh cream (roughly 2.8 g net per 100 ml, low-carb list) and unsweetened almond milk (under 1 g per 100 ml) are the standard swaps — cream for richness, almond milk for volume. Full-fat coconut milk in a tin works brilliantly for cooking curries and soups. Skip oat milk entirely; it is higher in carbs than dairy. For cheese lovers, hard cheeses like cheddar and gouda are low-carb because the lactose drains off with the whey during production.
